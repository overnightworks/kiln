# Six database orthodoxies crack, one stays unbuilt

Recent database research (2018–2026) is quietly abandoning three load-bearing assumptions of the field's last twenty years — that CPU-bound buffer-pool and locking work dominates OLTP cost, that interactive (multi-round-trip) transactions are common enough to justify non-deterministic engine designs, and that shared-nothing partitioning is the only scalable answer to cloud OLTP — while a fourth, that physical/logical data independence (keep the schema and query set open, let the optimizer cope) is the right default, is being challenged for the first time by workload-compiled engines. Against that backdrop, the "closed-world / workload-compiled database" sketched in the research question — one engine compiled once from a complete, known set of queries and invariants, with indexes, caches and subscriptions unified as incremental views, deterministic lock-free scheduling from static analysis, invariants replacing isolation levels, migrations computed as workload diffs, and compile-time cost bounds — turns out to be a **real combination of six separately-attested research lines, none of which has been fused with more than one other** in any system found. Parts 1 (workload-derived layout) and 2 (indexes-as-IVM) each have strong, named prior art (Bespoke OLAP/Castor; Materialize/DBSP/Noria) that stops short of the OLTP, same-transaction, no-separate-index-concept version proposed. Part 3 (static read/write-set derivation driving deterministic, lock-free, *abort-free* scheduling) has no match at all — every deterministic database discovers access sets at runtime. Part 4 (invariants replacing isolation) has a dense 1986–2022 lineage (escrow, I-confluence, Homeostasis, IPA, Hamsaz, Katara) that never reached a production relational engine or single-node many-core setting. Part 5 (migration as a compiled-workload diff) and part 6 (compile-time cost bounds per operation, with build-time rejection) are the thinnest: no source diffs two compiled workloads to derive a migration, and no system connects the mature compile-time resource-bound literature (AARA) to query engines. The strongest, best-evidenced claim in this report is therefore not that any single part is unprecedented, but that **no system combines even three of the six parts**, let alone all six — a conclusion held at medium confidence given the inherent limits of a time-boxed literature search.

## Communication, not computation, now bottlenecks OLTP — and three other orthodoxies are cracking

For roughly fifteen years the field treated H-Store's 2008 "Looking Glass" diagnosis — that buffer-pool management and concurrency control are the CPU-bound costs worth attacking — as settled. CIDR 2025's direct sequel, "OLTP Through the Looking Glass 16 Years Later," revisits that diagnosis on modern hardware and concludes **communication/messaging overhead between client and transaction logic now dominates**, worsened by the stronger process isolation multi-tenant security requires ([CIDR 2025 paper](https://www.vldb.org/cidrdb/papers/2025/p17-zhou.pdf)). This is a genuine reordering of what counts as the bottleneck, not an incremental finding — and it directly motivates engines that compile communication-light, server-resident procedures rather than chatty client-driven access patterns, which is exactly the posture the candidate system's workload-compiled approach assumes. A second cracking orthodoxy is the buffer pool itself: in-memory systems that hold the whole dataset in RAM skip it, and maintaining a buffer pool can consume **nearly a third of all CPU cycles** when data fits in memory ([TUM paper](https://www-db.cs.tum.edu/~zinsmeister/papers/predictive-translation.pdf)); the related "Are You Sure You Want to Use MMAP" CIDR 2022 paper documents mmap's correctness and performance pitfalls as a cautionary case where a hardware/OS shortcut was adopted before its costs were understood ([CMU paper](https://db.cs.cmu.edu/mmap-cidr2022/)) — a pattern repeated in the 2025/2026 io_uring literature, which found that simply swapping in io_uring does not reliably speed up a DBMS without matching engine-internal redesign ([arXiv 2512.04859](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.04859v1)). Third, "shared-nothing partitioning is the scalable default for cloud OLTP" is being displaced by convergence on shared, cache-coherent storage: CIDR 2023's "Is Scalable OLTP in the Cloud a Solved Problem?" argues the common single-writer shared-storage cloud pattern should move toward multi-writer, cache-coherent shared storage ([CIDR 2023 paper](https://mail.vldb.org/cidrdb/papers/2023/p50-ziegler.pdf)), and "zero-disk architecture" — object storage as the sole durability tier, local NVMe/RAM as pure cache — is now a named, deployed pattern (WarpStream, SlateDB, Turbopuffer, Neon) that explicitly discards "durability lives on local disk" as an assumption ([The New Stack on SlateDB](https://thenewstack.io/slatedb-bottomless-databases-built-on-cloud-object-stores/)). Fourth, and most load-bearing for the candidate system's premise, a rigorous 2026 empirical study of 111 open-source Django/TypeORM web applications found that **39% have zero interactive transactions**, that transactions strictly requiring external input mid-transaction are only about 0.5% of the workload, and that for 90% of applications at least 58% of transactions have fully inferable read/write sets up front — directly challenging the assumption that interactive transactions are common enough to force non-deterministic engine designs ([study reviewed by Demirbas](https://muratbuffalo.blogspot.com/2026/01/are-database-system-researchers-making.html)). A credible independent critique from the same review, however, notes this result is selection-biased toward ORM-driven web apps and does not generalize to enterprise or ad hoc analyst workloads, and that converting the remaining interactive transactions to one-shot form is not the "minimal change" the original authors claim — so this challenge should be read as real but narrower than its headline, a live and contested finding rather than settled fact. Deterministic simulation testing (DST) — diffused from FoundationDB's internal tooling into TigerBeetle's VOPR and the commercial product Antithesis, and now adopted by Turso's from-scratch SQLite rewrite — represents a fifth, more methodological shift: it is displacing "we fault-injected it with Jepsen" as the bar for correctness evidence in new storage engines ([TigerBeetle VOPR docs](https://docs.tigerbeetle.com/about/vopr)).

## Parts 1 through 3 each have a named closest ancestor, but none reaches OLTP generality

**Part 1 (workload-derived indexes and layout).** The closest matches form a clean trajectory: Data Calculator and its successors Cosine and Limousine (SIGMOD 2024) synthesize a *single* key-value data structure from a workload description using learned cost models, but remain design-space exploration tools layered on an outer loop, not whole-engine compilers ([CIDR 2019 Design Continuums](https://mail.vldb.org/cidrdb/papers/2019/p143-idreos-cidr19.pdf)). Horticulture and Schism derive partitioning plans from a *sample* workload for the H-Store/VoltDB lineage, but only partitioning — not indexes, layout, and execution code together — and with no formal completeness guarantee ([H-Store partitioning paper](https://hstore.cs.brown.edu/papers/hstore-partitioning.pdf)). **Bespoke OLAP** (PVLDB vol. 19, 2026) is the closest system found to "compile a whole engine from a closed workload": an LLM-agent pipeline that takes a schema, query templates with parameter ranges, and a sample dataset, and synthesizes storage layouts, hash indexes, and sorted range directories from scratch, reporting order-of-magnitude speedups over DuckDB ([arXiv 2603.02001](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.02001v1)). It is, however, **OLAP-only, single-threaded, in-memory, gives no cost bound, and explicitly treats transactions/concurrency as future work** — and a direct 2026 rebuttal, JetStream, criticizes exactly this "rewrite the base data representation" premise as complicating writes, proposing instead accelerators layered on an unmodified DBMS that preserve support for ad-hoc (non-templated) queries ([arXiv 2608.22476](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.22476)) — a live internal debate about whether the closed-world premise itself is sound. Confidence that no OLTP-capable, formally-bounded analogue exists: **medium**, given a broad but non-exhaustive search.

**Part 2 (every query as an incrementally maintained view, unifying index/cache/materialized-view/subscription).** This is the best-attested part individually. DBSP/Feldera (VLDB 2023 Best Paper) gives a general, heuristic-free incrementalization algorithm — Z-sets with signed multiplicities make deletion "free" and joins bilinear — claimed to cover arbitrary SQL, Datalog, and even stratified recursion uniformly ([arXiv 2203.16684](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.16684)), but Feldera's own docs state plainly that "DBSP is not designed to replace a standard database" — it sits downstream of a system of record. Materialize's **arrangements** genuinely unify the internal mechanism behind indexes and materialized views, sharing one incrementally-maintained structure across queries ([Materialize docs](https://materialize.com/docs/overview/arrangements)), yet its own documentation still exposes `CREATE INDEX` and `CREATE MATERIALIZED VIEW` as two distinct user-facing object types with different durability semantics — the implementation is unified, the concept is not. Noria (OSDI 2018) comes closest to the unification being load-bearing: partial-state operators gate materialization on whether a cache-miss can be served by an index lookup, and it reports **5× the load** of a hand-optimized MySQL baseline on a real application ([OSDI'18](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi18/presentation/gjengset)) — but its guarantee is only eventual consistency, and its commercial descendant ReadySet sits downstream of Postgres/MySQL via logical replication, requiring manual `CACHE QUERY` opt-in rather than treating every query uniformly. **pg_ivm** is the one documented case of view maintenance running inside the same transaction as the triggering write in a mainstream OLTP engine (Postgres), via an `AFTER` trigger ([github.com/sraoss/pg_ivm](https://github.com/sraoss/pg_ivm)) — but it is an opt-in extension, coexists with ordinary B-tree indexes rather than replacing them, and SQL Server's indexed views show why the restriction exists: outer joins are disallowed outright because rows can "logically disappear" from an outer-joined view on insert, which the documented incremental-maintenance algorithm cannot handle efficiently ([sqlshack.com](https://www.sqlshack.com/sql-server-indexed-views)). No source found treats a secondary index as literally a special case of a parameterized IVM view with the index concept removed, and no source combines DBSP-level generality (arbitrary deletes, joins, aggregates, recursion) with native, same-serializable-transaction maintenance inside an OLTP engine as the *only* access path — this absence is held at **medium** confidence.

**Part 3 (static read/write-set analysis enabling deterministic, lock-free, abort-free parallel scheduling).** This is the part with the weakest prior art and the strongest-held novelty claim in the entire report. Every deterministic database surveyed — Calvin, Bohm, Aria, Caracal, ForeSight, Block-STM, Solana Sealevel, Sui — obtains its read/write sets by one of two runtime mechanisms, never by static analysis of compiled code: either the access set is *declared* up front by the transaction author (Sealevel's declared accounts, Sui's object ownership, Calvin/Bohm's a-priori requirement), or it is *discovered* by actually executing — a "reconnaissance query" run before the real transaction (Calvin) or a full speculative execution pass later validated or re-executed on conflict (Aria, Block-STM) ([Aria paper](https://www.cs.arizona.edu/~caolei/papers/Aria.pdf); [Block-STM](https://web3.arxiv.org/abs/2203.06871v3)). The one source found that automatically derives read/write-set approximations from source code — Amato, Giannotti & Mainetto, VLDB 1993 — does so via runtime abstract interpretation of an interpreted language feeding a conventional two-phase-locking scheduler, not build-time compilation, and predates and was never picked up by the Calvin lineage ([vldb.org 1993](https://www.vldb.org/conf/1993/P405.PDF)). Critically, every system's reported throughput gain (1.9×–9.7× for Caracal, up to 2× for Aria, up to 2× over Aria for ForeSight under skew) is explicitly qualified "under contention" — skew and hot-key resilience, not raw throughput, is what differentiates designs in this literature, and dependent reads (lookup-by-email-then-decide) remain the universally hard case that every system resolves only via a genuine runtime step. A static-analysis system deriving read/write sets purely as a function of a transaction's *parameters*, at build time, sufficient for a lock-free and specifically **abort-free** (not merely deterministic-commit-with-re-execution) parallel schedule, was matched by **no** system found — held at medium-high confidence that this is a genuine gap rather than a search miss, since the query budget did not include a full DBLP/ACM-DL crawl.

## Invariants, migrations and cost bounds are where prior art thins out fastest

**Part 4 (invariants instead of isolation levels).** Here the literature is dense but consistently off-target for the candidate system's setting. A continuous 1986–2022 lineage — O'Neil's escrow method → Bailis et al.'s invariant confluence (I-confluence, PVLDB 2015) → Homeostasis (SIGMOD 2015) → Indigo/Explicit Consistency (EuroSys 2015) → IPA (VLDB 2019) → CISE/Hamsaz/Hampa → Katara (PLDI/OOPSLA 2022) → ECROs (OOPSLA 2021) — all do precisely what the candidate proposes: take a declared invariant, statically decide which operations can run coordination-free without ever violating it, and coordinate only the residual cases. Bailis's own framing is the clearest statement of both the opportunity and the hard limit: "without knowing the application's invariants, the best you can do under a read/write model is serializability," but "if an invariant is not I-confluent, no coordination-free strategy exists" — the canonical non-confluent example being a decrement-only counter that must stay non-negative ([Bailis blog](https://www.bailis.org/blog/when-does-consistency-require-coordination/)). Their TPC-C case study found **10 of 12 benchmark invariants are I-confluent**, letting a coordination-avoiding prototype reach roughly 12.7M New-Order tx/s across 200 servers — a 25× improvement over prior distributed results ([arXiv 1402.2237](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1402.2237)). Two independent threads — Fekete et al.'s 2008 write-skew analysis and a 2024 critique of naive snapshot-isolation constraint checking — cross-validate the theoretical limit from the opposite direction: checking a predicate independently per transaction, without coordination, is unsound for exactly the invariant class I-confluence would also flag as non-confluent ([arXiv 2405.18393](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.18393)). But every system in this entire lineage was built as a prototype or middleware layered on a geo-replicated CRDT store (AntidoteDB/Walter-style); **none was found integrated into a production, general-purpose relational engine**, and — more specifically to the candidate system's stated goal — **no source applies this reasoning to single-node, many-core concurrency control** at all. The whole tradition treats "coordination" as a cross-replica network round trip, with reported wins measured against WAN/consensus latency, not against lock contention or cache-line conflicts within one machine; a candidate claiming single-node many-core gains from invariant-driven coordination would be building on a cost model this literature has simply never addressed. This is flagged as a genuine, reasonably well-evidenced gap rather than a search artifact (medium-high confidence). TigerBeetle's "≥2 assertions per function" discipline is the closest cultural analog, but it is defensive coding on a fixed ledger schema, not a concurrency-control mechanism.

**Part 5 (migrations as a compiler diff between workload versions, backfilled from the log).** The field cleanly splits into schema-diff tooling (gh-ost, pt-online-schema-change, Google F1's provably-safe asynchronous schema-change sequencing, CockroachDB's descent from F1, Atlas/Skeema/Bytebase, and the academic line PRISM/InVerDa/Bullfrog/Tesseract) that diffs table/column DDL and backfills data to match, versus schema-less log-replay systems (XTDB, Datomic) that treat migration as an ad hoc backfill script over an immutable history with no compiled diff at all ([F1 paper](https://research.google/pubs/online-asynchronous-schema-change-in-f1/); [InVerDa/BiDEL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1608.05564)). Noria is the only system found whose migration story is query/dataflow-centric rather than DDL-centric — it "provides fast schema migrations" by reusing existing materialized dataflow state and retaining the raw write log for rollback — but no retrievable source explains whether a migration is *computed* as a diff of two compiled query sets versus manually specified by a developer adding a query ([MIT PDOS Noria overview](https://vm-web.pdos.csail.mit.edu/projects/noria.html)). **No system or paper found derives a migration from a diff of the application's compiled queries/transactions themselves** and backfills purely from that diff against the log — this is the single cleanest "absent from the literature" finding in this research, held at medium-high confidence, built from parts (schema-diff tooling, log backfill, per-query incremental materialization) that each exist only in isolation.

**Part 6 (compile-time cost bounds per operation).** This is the thinnest part of all six. No system gives a true static worst-case cost bound per operation in the sense of "this query is provably O(f(n)) at build time, or rejected." Castor (the closest layout-and-query co-synthesis system, for read-only data against a static dataset) uses a cost model only to *guide search*, and its own paper documents the consequence directly: the optimizer "can produce layouts too large to be compiled efficiently" — a failure observed empirically, never precluded by proof ([arXiv 1903.03229](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1903.03229)). Bespoke OLAP "sidesteps cost modeling entirely," choosing implementations by empirical benchmarking with regression rollback rather than any bound, static or dynamic. Separately, Automatic Amortized Resource Analysis (AARA) is a mature, type-system-based body of work that *does* prove compile-time worst-case resource bounds for functional and imperative programs, with soundness proofs and extensions into Rust and even quantum circuits as of 2025 ([AARA survey](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/9A47A8663CD8A7147E2F17865C368094/S0960129521000487a.pdf/two-decades-of-automatic-amortized-resource-analysis.pdf)) — but this search found **no paper connecting AARA, or an equivalent static bound technique, to a database query language or engine**. The only adjacent practical mechanism, Greenplum's `statement_cost` threshold, rejects a query using the optimizer's cost *estimate*, which the PostgreSQL community has explicitly resisted formalizing because those estimates are known to be unreliable. The two research threads — formal compile-time resource bounds, and workload-compiled/specialized databases — appear, from this search, simply never to have been connected (medium-high confidence).

## Combination search: no system fuses more than two of the six parts

Running the full six-part description against the literature surfaces individual best-fit precedents per part but **no system that combines more than two**: Bespoke OLAP/JetStream for part 1 (compile-time workload specialization, OLAP-only); Zero/Feldera/Noria/Convex for part 2 (IVM-backed queries, generally with developer-declared rather than workload-derived indexes); VoltDB for part 3 (deterministic, statically-partitioned scheduling, but partitioning is developer-declared, not workload-derived, and cross-partition work is serialized through a coordinator rather than parallelized); no system at all for part 4, with TigerBeetle as a weak cultural analog only. VoltDB in particular is worth dwelling on as the field's actual closest *mechanism* match for part 3's determinism/lock-freedom goal — it achieves both by sidestepping the general problem entirely, partitioning data so single-partition stored procedures run lock-free on dedicated single-threaded engines, while multi-partition transactions are serialized (not parallelized) through a Multi-Partition Initiator whose throughput degrades with node count ([Jepsen VoltDB 6.3](https://jepsen.io/analyses/voltdb-6-3)). Turso's `BEGIN CONCURRENT` mode, shipped on Turso Cloud in August 2026, is architecturally the opposite reference point: MVCC with row-level conflict detection at commit, returning `SQLITE_BUSY` for the application to retry — abort-based optimism, not deterministic lock-freedom ([Turso blog](https://turso.tech/blog/concurrent-writes-in-practice)). DBOS (VLDB 2022) is a conceptually adjacent but distinct idea — running OS services as transactions on a DBMS rather than compiling an application's workload into a specialized engine — and SpacetimeDB (WASM modules with reducers/procedures/views, IVM-like subscription push) and Convex (OCC-based reactive re-execution rather than true incremental maintenance) both sit in the OLTP/reactive-app lineage without workload-derived physical design or static scheduling. The "closed-world" framing itself is also absent from the literature in this sense: the term already has an established, unrelated meaning in database theory (the closed-world assumption about missing facts, Reiter 1977) that no source conflates with "complete query/transaction set known at build time" — suggesting the candidate system's framing is either genuinely novel vocabulary or simply not yet indexed under a searched term. Taken together — six parts, each independently attested by one to several research lines, zero systems combining more than two — the overall synthesis is best read as a **plausible, well-motivated architectural proposal that would need to be built essentially from scratch**, not an assembly of existing subsystems; confidence in this combination-level novelty claim is medium, bounded by the search's inability to complete a full VLDB/SIGMOD/CIDR proceedings crawl.

## Eight open problems define the field's 2026 frontier

Synthesizing gaps named independently across every research thread above, eight directions stand out as the most promising and underexplored in OLTP/embedded database architecture as of 2026. First, **invariant-driven coordination avoidance has never been applied to single-node, many-core concurrency control** — the entire I-confluence/Homeostasis/IPA lineage treats coordination as a WAN round trip, leaving lock-contention and cache-coherency cost models completely unaddressed by this literature, a clean and currently unclaimed research niche. Second, **dependent reads remain the universal weak point of static/deterministic scheduling** — no system derives a dependent access pattern (lookup-by-secondary-key-then-write) without a genuine runtime step, and no source even discusses how a parametric static derivation would compose with non-key lookups at build time. Third, **migration-as-compiled-workload-diff is entirely unbuilt**: the schema-diff and log-replay camps have never been connected to per-query incremental materialization in one mechanism. Fourth, **no one has pointed mature static resource-bound analysis (AARA) at a query language or engine**, despite AARA being a two-decade-old, soundness-proven technique actively extended into new domains (Rust, quantum circuits) every year. Fifth, the **interactive-transactions debate needs a broader, non-ORM-biased replication** — Demirbas's own critique explicitly calls for real engineering-cost data on converting interactive transactions to one-shot form across enterprise and ad hoc workloads, not just open-source Django/TypeORM apps, a concretely scoped and tractable empirical project. Sixth, **deterministic simulation testing has not yet been retrofitted onto most existing embedded/local-first engines** — only FoundationDB, TigerBeetle, and Turso/Limbo have adopted it as of 2026, leaving a wide field of storage engines still relying on conventional fault injection. Seventh, **agent-first data system redesign** is emerging as a plausible new workload class — Neon reports 80% of its databases are now created by agents, and a CIDR 2026 Berkeley paper argues "agentic speculation" (scale, heterogeneity, redundancy, steerability) should drive native redesign of query interfaces and memory stores ([arXiv 2509.00997](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.00997)) — but as of October 2026 this remains vision-paper and vendor-anecdote stage, unvalidated by independent measurement of production agent traffic. Eighth, **zero-disk architecture's actual latency/durability tradeoffs lack any peer-reviewed benchmark** — current evidence for WarpStream/SlateDB/Turbopuffer-style object-storage-as-sole-durability designs is vendor blogs and tech journalism only, leaving a concrete, well-specified measurement gap for independent researchers. A ninth, narrower item worth naming: the **io_uring and CXL hardware-substrate literature consistently shows that swapping in new I/O or memory hardware without matching engine-internal redesign underdelivers** — a pattern now demonstrated three times independently (mmap 2022, io_uring 2025/2026, CXL buffer-pool proposals) — suggesting the next profitable work is narrow, measured "when exactly does this help" studies for a specific engine rather than further hardware-substrate proposals in the abstract.

The throughline across all eight is that the field's hardest unsolved problems are not about inventing new mechanisms — escrow, I-confluence, AARA, DST, and workload-compiled layout synthesis all already exist — but about **connecting mechanisms that were each developed for a different setting** (geo-replication, OLAP, functional-program analysis, blockchain) to the single-node, many-core, transactional, invariant-rich setting the candidate "closed-world" database targets. That reframing is itself the report's most useful takeaway: the candidate system's novelty is not in any one of its six parts but in treating them as one coherent design problem, which is precisely the move no prior system has made.
