# OLTP and Embedded Database Architecture: Open Problems and Challenged Assumptions (2020-2026)

Scope note: this covers OLTP and embedded/local-first database architecture specifically. Coverage is uneven where search results were sparse (noted per section as Gaps). All claims are dated; anything pre-2020 is marked as background only.

## What recent keynotes, vision papers, and surveys name as open problems (CIDR, Stonebraker/Pavlo 2024, Seattle Report, buffer pool/MMAP debates)

### Takeaway
The community's own vision papers converge on three themes: (1) cloud/disaggregated OLTP architecture is still unsettled — multiple competing answers to "where does state live and who writes it," (2) the 2007-era assumption that CPU-bound buffer-pool/concurrency-control work is the OLTP bottleneck is now explicitly being revised in favor of communication/network overhead, and (3) mmap-as-buffer-pool-substitute is a documented anti-pattern with an "I told you so" paper trail. No 2025/2026 successor to the 2019 Seattle Report was found — the field's major periodic self-assessment appears not to have been refreshed since the 2022 CACM publication of the 2018 meeting's findings.

### Cited Findings
- Stonebraker and Pavlo's "What Goes Around Comes Around... And Around" (SIGMOD Record 53.2, 2024, pp. 21-37) is a follow-up to Stonebraker & Hellerstein's 2005 "What Goes Around Comes Around," covering roughly the last 20 years of data-model/architecture churn. — [SIGMOD Record page](https://sigmodrecord.org/2024/06/30/what-goes-around-comes-around-and-around); [UBC course citation](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~rap/teaching/504/2025W2/topics/goesaround.shtml). Full content (its specific list of open problems) was not retrievable from search snippets alone — see Gaps.
- "Are You Sure You Want to Use MMAP in Your Database Management System?" (Crotty, Leis, Pavlo; CIDR 2022) argues mmap has "severe correctness and performance issues" not obvious up front; several popular systems adopted mmap for larger-than-memory data and later paid real engineering cost moving to self-managed file I/O. The paper ends with a "prescription" for when mmap use is still reasonable (exact conditions not retrieved). — [CMU paper page](https://db.cs.cmu.edu/mmap-cidr2022/); [CIDR proceedings](https://vldb.org/cidrdb/2022/are-you-sure-you-want-to-use-mmap-in-your-database-management-system.html)
- The paper drew public pushback: RavenDB published a response, and Symas/LMDB's Howard Chu wrote a 2024 rebuttal agreeing with RavenDB that the benchmarks compare unlike systems and omit some mmap-based alternatives. — [Symas rebuttal](https://www.symas.com/post/are-you-sure-you-want-to-use-mmap-in-your-dbms); [Ayende/RavenDB response](https://ayende.com/blog/196161-c/re-are-you-sure-you-want-to-use-mmap-in-your-database-management-system)
- On "is the buffer pool still needed": in-memory DBMSs that hold the whole dataset in RAM omit a buffer pool entirely; one cited paper reports that maintaining a buffer pool can cost nearly one-third of all CPU cycles when data fits in memory. Falling-then-stagnating DRAM prices plus cheap flash SSDs pushed some systems back toward SSD-optimized designs with a lightweight buffer manager. Most DBMSs still use STEAL+NO-FORCE buffer pool policies tied to WAL-based recovery. — [TUM paper](https://www-db.cs.tum.edu/~zinsmeister/papers/predictive-translation.pdf); [Georgia Tech slides](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~jarulraj/courses/4420-s19/slides/02-inmemory.pdf) (confidence: medium — sourced from course materials and a non-primary synthesis, not a single authoritative survey)
- CIDR 2023, "Is Scalable OLTP in the Cloud a Solved Problem?" (Ziegler, Bernstein, Leis, Binnig): argues that single-writer shared-storage designs (the common cloud OLTP pattern) depart from the shared-nothing orthodoxy, and proposes a multi-writer, cache-coherent shared-storage design as a promising path, explicitly listing open research challenges. Authors' own slides frame the title's answer as "no, but there's a path." — [CIDR page](https://vldb.org/cidrdb/2023/is-scalable-oltp-in-the-cloud-a-solved-problem.html); [paper PDF](https://mail.vldb.org/cidrdb/papers/2023/p50-ziegler.pdf)
- CIDR 2025, "OLTP Through the Looking Glass 16 Years Later: Communication is the New Bottleneck" (Zhou, Leis, Yu, Stonebraker; MIT/TU Munich/UW-Madison): revisits the 2008 H-Store "Looking Glass" paper, which identified buffer-pool management and concurrency control as the dominant CPU-bound OLTP bottlenecks. The 2025 paper's central claim is that on modern hardware, communication/messaging overhead between client and stored-procedure/transaction logic now dominates, and that stronger isolation of user-defined code (needed for multi-tenant security) raises communication cost further. — [CIDR page](https://vldb.org/cidrdb/2025/oltp-through-the-looking-glass-16-years-later-communication-is-the-new-bottleneck.html); [paper PDF](https://www.vldb.org/cidrdb/papers/2025/p17-zhou.pdf)
- CIDR 2025, "Pasha" (Huang et al.): proposes a database architecture for CXL memory pods that gives each host ownership of its partitions specifically to avoid multi-host transactions and two-phase commit. — [dbscholar entry](https://rmarcus.info/dbscholar/papers/h385e6e60ac0ef17a)
- The Seattle Report on Database Research was published in CACM 65(8), 2022, pp. 72-79, but reflects a meeting held in Seattle in fall 2018 plus SIGMOD/VLDB 2020 panel discussion — i.e., it is already stale relative to 2026 by design (5-year cadence). Themes included data cleaning/transformation for data science pipelines and disaggregated engine architectures for multitenant cloud services. No 2025 successor report was found in search results. — [CACM record](https://cacm.acm.org/?p=51522); [EPFL record](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/296715)
- Andy Pavlo's "Databases in 2025: A Year in Review" (CMU blog, posted Jan 4, 2026) states PostgreSQL is "the only major DBMS still relying on the OS page cache" rather than a self-managed buffer pool, calling this a known, unresolved gap; PostgreSQL 18's new async-I/O subsystem is presented as the first step toward closing it. — [Pavlo blog listing](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~pavlo/blog); reported via [Postgres Weekly #630](https://postgresweekly.com/issues/630)
- Same Pavlo 2025 review: three competing PostgreSQL sharding projects are active in parallel (Multigres/Supabase, Neki/PlanetScale, PgDog), which Pavlo frames as not architecturally new — citing dormant precedents Postgres-XC and Postgres-XL (inactive since 2018) and Citus (originally OLAP-focused). YugabyteDB is a hard fork compatible only up to Postgres v15; Aurora Limitless is closed-source. — same source

### Inferences
- The field's own framing has shifted from "how do we make transactions execute fast" (buffer pool, locking, logging — the 2008 Looking Glass concerns) to "how do we make transactions communicate fast" (network/RPC overhead, multi-tenant isolation cost) — a genuine reordering of what counts as the OLTP bottleneck, not just an incremental finding.
- The repeated, independent arrival at "shared storage, single or multi-writer, cache-coherent" as the answer to cloud OLTP scalability (Ziegler et al. 2023, and implicitly Neon/Aurora/HorizonDB per Pavlo's 2025 review) suggests this is the converging industry consensus, displacing pure shared-nothing partitioning as the default scalable architecture — though sharding projects for Postgres (Multigres, Neki, PgDog) show shared-nothing is far from abandoned in practice.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve the actual numbered list of open problems from Stonebraker & Pavlo 2024 itself (only metadata/citation info surfaced; the PDF content was not fetched).
- No 2025/2026 successor to the Seattle Report was found; it's unclear whether one is planned, or whether CIDR/VLDB keynotes have informally taken over that role.
- VLDB/SIGMOD keynote content for 2023-2026 specifically was not searched directly (only indirectly via Pavlo's blog and CIDR papers) — a direct search of keynote slide decks/abstracts would strengthen this section.

## Hardware-driven rethinks: NVMe/io_uring, CXL, disaggregated/object-storage-native engines, many-core

### Takeaway
Three hardware shifts are visibly reshaping OLTP engine design as of 2025-2026: (1) io_uring is being adopted but recent peer-reviewed work shows it is not a free performance win and requires careful engine-level redesign; (2) CXL memory pooling/disaggregation has moved from pure proposal (2024) to a SIGMOD 2025 working system (PolarCXLMem) and cloud GA availability (Microsoft, Nov 2025 per a vendor source); (3) "zero-disk architecture" (object storage as the only durable tier, with local NVMe/RAM as pure cache) is a named, actively-deployed pattern (WarpStream, SlateDB, Turbopuffer, Neon) with real production tradeoffs around latency.

### Cited Findings
- "High-Performance DBMSs with io_uring: When and How to use it" (Jasny, Leis, Binnig et al.; arXiv Dec 2025, VLDB 2026) integrates io_uring into a transactional storage engine on NVMe SSDs and concludes that simply swapping in io_uring does not reliably speed up a DBMS — it requires careful use. The authors report improving PostgreSQL's io_uring backend for a >10% gain (one secondary source reports 14%). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.04859v1); [dbscholar summary](https://rmarcus.info/dbscholar/papers/hd669e644a9fb5089)
- PostgreSQL 18 shipped an `io_method` setting with `io_uring` as a Linux option, aimed at improving throughput on NVMe/SSD storage. — [Xata glossary](https://xata.io/glossary/async-io); corroborated by Pavlo's 2025 year-in-review noting Postgres 18's async-I/O subsystem as a first step away from OS-page-cache reliance.
- A CIDR 2026 paper integrates xNVMe (a more flexible I/O backend) into DuckDB, aiming at more flexible NVMe I/O than raw io_uring/libaio bindings. — [DuckDB blog](https://duckdb.org/library/flexible-io-xnvme/)
- "CXL and the Return of Scale-Up Database Engines" (arXiv 2401.01150, 2024) proposes putting the buffer pool in CXL-attached memory while keeping query processing on local DRAM, letting new query engines start without a warm-up period and letting threads/engines migrate between servers because data stays in a shared pool. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.01150); [literature review](https://www.themoonlight.io/review/cxl-and-the-return-of-scale-up-database-engines)
- Motivation for CXL pooling: "stranded memory" (DRAM installed but underused per-server) is cited at roughly 60% fleet-level utilization in one industry source, and Azure research is cited elsewhere as finding up to 25% of memory stranded (confidence: low-medium — vendor/blog sourced, not a peer-reviewed measurement in the results retrieved). — [SNIA SDC22 slides](https://www.snia.org/sites/default/files/2025-05/SNIA-SDC22-Fan-Memory-Disaggregation-and-Pooling-with-CXL.pdf)
- SIGMOD 2025, "Unlocking the Potential of CXL for Disaggregated Memory in Cloud-Native Databases" (PolarCXLMem) is a working CXL-switch design for cloud-native databases, reporting reduced amplification, higher bandwidth, and up to 2x the throughput of RDMA-based PolarDB. This is the most concrete evidence that CXL-for-databases has moved from proposal to working prototype with measured gains over an RDMA baseline. — [dbscholar entry](https://rmarcus.info/dbscholar/papers/hc634484319399e4a)
- A vendor source (Introl) claims Microsoft launched CXL-equipped cloud instances in November 2025 and that CXL 4.0 doubles bandwidth to 128 GT/s, projecting a ~$15B CXL market by 2028 (confidence: low — single vendor blog, figures not cross-checked). — [Introl blog](https://introl.com/blog/cxl-memory-expansion-pooling-disaggregated-memory-ai-data-center-2025)
- "Zero-Disk Architecture" (ZDA): object storage (e.g., S3) as the sole durability tier, with local NVMe/RAM purely as cache, is explicitly named and credited to WarpStream's design (acquired by Confluent), and is being replicated by SlateDB (a KV store with high write latency but "infinite" scalability) and others. — [SlateDB coverage, The New Stack](https://thenewstack.io/slatedb-bottomless-databases-built-on-cloud-object-stores/)
- Turbopuffer (vector/full-text search DB): keeps S3/GCS as the single source of truth, memory+NVMe as a cache tier only; uses a clustered-index design specifically because graph indexes like HNSW need many small round trips that are expensive against object storage. — [Turbopuffer architecture writeup, Sept 2025](https://jxnl.co/writing/2025/09/11/turbopuffer-object-storage-first-vector-database-architecture/)
- Neon is described (secondary source) as serverless PostgreSQL storing page data on S3; cost argument for ZDA designs generally cites avoided cross-zone transfer fees (one WarpStream post cites $0.022/GiB cross-zone transfer) and reduced ops burden; the acknowledged tradeoff across all these systems is added latency, which WarpStream states many users accept for lower operating cost. — [The New Stack, object store apps](https://thenewstack.io/object-store-apps-cloud-natives-freshest-architecture/)
- Pavlo's 2025 year-in-review: Neon, Microsoft HorizonDB, and Amazon Aurora all use a single primary node with compute/storage separation; Snowflake's Postgres service instead uses standard Postgres on Crunchy Bridge (i.e., not disaggregated storage) — showing disaggregation is a design choice, not yet a converged consensus even among hyperscaler-adjacent products.

### Inferences
- The io_uring and CXL findings together suggest the "just change the I/O/memory substrate" approach to modernizing OLTP engines consistently underdelivers without matching engine-internal redesign — a recurring pattern across the mmap paper (2022), the io_uring paper (2025/2026), and implicitly the CXL buffer-pool proposal (which requires a new engine architecture, not a drop-in change).
- Zero-disk/object-storage-native architecture is the clearest *embedded-adjacent* hardware rethink for OLTP infra (not embedded databases themselves, but the infra embedded/serverless databases like Neon run on) — it directly challenges the "separate caching layer is distinct from durable storage" and "fsync-based local durability" assumptions by making the cache tier explicitly ephemeral and object storage (with its own consistency model) the durability boundary.

### Gaps
- Found no rigorous, peer-reviewed benchmark comparing ZDA systems' actual latency/durability tradeoffs against traditional replicated-disk architectures — current evidence is vendor blogs and secondary tech journalism (The New Stack).
- Many-core OLTP (explicitly requested in the key questions) was not directly searched — no findings on 2024-2026 many-core-specific OLTP scaling work (e.g., NUMA-aware engines, work on 100+ core scaling) are included here. This is a clear gap; a follow-up search on "many-core OLTP scaling 2025" is recommended.
- CXL figures (stranded memory %, market size, bandwidth specs) rely on vendor sources not cross-verified against the CXL Consortium or independent benchmarks.

## Software-driven rethinks: deterministic simulation testing, embedded/local-first databases, interactive transactions, AI/LLM effects on DB design and tuning

### Takeaway
Deterministic simulation testing (DST) has diffused from FoundationDB's internal tooling into a named industry category (TigerBeetle's VOPR, Antithesis as a commercial product, Turso/Limbo adopting it for a from-scratch SQLite rewrite), directly challenging "fsync-based local durability" and ad hoc fault-injection testing as sufficient correctness tooling. Separately, a rigorous 2026 empirical study directly challenges the "interactive transactions are common and essential" assumption behind non-deterministic OLTP designs — but a credible independent critique (Murat Demirbas) shows the challenge is narrower than its title claims. SQLite-lineage embedded databases (Turso/Limbo) and the broader embedded/local-first wave are real and growing but mostly pre-revenue/early-stage as of 2025. Evidence on LLM/AI effects on database design is thin and mostly vendor-blog-level, except for two more rigorous threads: Neon's reported 80%-of-databases-created-by-agents statistic, and emerging VLDB/CIDR 2026 papers proposing "agent-first" data system redesigns.

### Cited Findings
- Deterministic simulation testing (DST) origin: FoundationDB built a simulation-first testing framework to validate distributed-database correctness by making every nondeterministic component pluggable; this approach is described as the conceptual ancestor of Antithesis's commercial product. — [DST overview](https://antithesis.com/docs/resources/deterministic_simulation_testing/)
- TigerBeetle's simulator "VOPR" runs production TigerBeetle code under varied, seeded conditions so a found bug reproduces exactly from its seed and git commit; VOPR can compress time such that one minute of VOPR time represents days of real-world testing. A TigerBeetle engineer has also described a "protocol-aware" DST variant giving the harness visibility into consensus/storage state, which the source contrasts with black-box tools like Jepsen. — [TigerBeetle VOPR docs](https://docs.tigerbeetle.com/about/vopr); [Antithesis bugbash talk](https://antithesis.com/bugbash/talks/protocol-aware-deterministic-simulation-testing/)
- Antithesis runs ordinary, non-rewritten software inside a deterministic hypervisor rather than requiring an FDB-style from-scratch rewrite; one market analysis argues Antithesis's main competition is companies' own in-house simulators, not other vendors, implying most serious distributed-systems shops already build some form of DST internally. — [Sacra analysis](https://sacra.com/chat/h/a31c3f5a-3f58-4aaf-b8e2-32a2afca32a2/)
- Turso's 2025 ground-up Rust rewrite of SQLite ("Limbo") was pursued partly because async I/O and memory safety were judged easier to achieve outside SQLite's C codebase; a 2025 Changelog interview with Turso's CEO covers Limbo's adoption of deterministic simulation testing as part of its correctness strategy. Limbo was still early-stage as of a 2025 report. — [dbdb.io Turso entry](https://dbdb.io/db/turso/revisions/3); [Changelog interview](https://audioscrape.com/podcast/the-changelog-software-development-open-source/episode/turso-is-rewriting-sqlite-in-rust-interview)
- A single Russian-language source (SecurityLab, confidence: low, unverified against a second source) reports Turso began building a PostgreSQL-compatible variant in July 2026, with the founder comparing the shared engine's ambition to LLVM and suggesting MySQL/Redis-compatible front ends could follow. — [SecurityLab](https://www.securitylab.ru/news/575458.php)
- "Are Database System Researchers Making Correct Assumptions about Transaction Workloads?" (Murat Demirbas blog, Jan 5, 2026), reviewing an ACM paper (doi 10.1145/3725268) that studied 111 popular open-source Django/TypeORM web apps with 30,000+ transactions:
  - 39% of the apps have zero interactive transactions; in the remaining 61%, interactive transactions are only ~9.6% of the workload.
  - Transactions strictly requiring external input or side effects mid-transaction are only ~0.5% of the workload; the paper's authors argue most other "interactive" transactions can be converted to one-shot form with minimal code changes.
  - For 90% of studied applications, at least 58% of transactions have fully inferable read/write sets up front — the key precondition deterministic DBs (Calvin, SLOG, Detock) need.
  - ~27% of transactions query by a secondary attribute (e.g., email) rather than the primary/locking key, making the read/write set not directly inferable; the paper's authors consider a cheap "reconnaissance query" fallback acceptable since these are usually simple single-statement transactions.
  — [Demirbas blog](https://muratbuffalo.blogspot.com/2026/01/are-database-system-researchers-making.html)
- Demirbas's own critique of that paper (same post, confidence: this is informed opinion from a recognized distributed-systems researcher, not an independent re-study): (1) selection bias — open-source ORM-based web apps exclude most enterprise systems and ad hoc DBA/analyst transactions, which are disproportionately interactive and often performance-critical; (2) the claim that non-strictly-interactive transactions convert easily via a scout/dry-run read phase is "optimistic" and lacks real engineering-cost data; (3) the paper's broad title overclaims — it only challenges assumptions within the deterministic-database niche (Calvin/SLOG/Detock), not standard locking/MVCC systems like PostgreSQL or Spanner, which never relied on those assumptions in the first place.
- On AI/LLM effects on database design: Pavlo's 2025 year-in-review states every major DBMS added an MCP (Model Context Protocol) server in 2025, which he characterizes as "simple proxies with no deep introspection of requests," and stresses least-privilege accounts plus enterprise guardrails (citing IBM Guardium, Oracle Database Firewall) as the current mitigation. He reports Neon's own claim that agents create 80% of its databases, which Neon uses to justify branching as a key feature for agent workflows. — [reported via Postgres Weekly #630](https://postgresweekly.com/issues/630) summarizing Pavlo's CMU blog
- "Supporting Our AI Overlords: Redesigning Data Systems to be Agent-First" (Liu et al., UC Berkeley, led by Aditya Parameswaran and Joseph Gonzalez; arXiv 2509.00997, CIDR 2026, Chaminade, Jan 18-21 2026): argues LLM-agent-driven data manipulation/analysis — termed "agentic speculation," characterized by scale, heterogeneity, redundancy, and steerability — will likely become the dominant workload for data systems, and that current systems' query interfaces, query processing, and memory stores need native redesign to support it. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.00997); [CIDR 2026 page](https://vldb.org/cidrdb/2026/supporting-our-ai-overlords-redesigning-data-systems-to-be-agent-first.html)
- Related: "Data Agents: Rethinking Data Systems in the AI Agent Era" is a VLDB 2026 panel reframing data systems around autonomous agents that perceive heterogeneous data, reason over semantics, plan multi-step analyses, and collaborate with users — content not independently retrieved beyond the title/listing. — [dbscholar listing](https://rmarcus.info/dbscholar/papers/h42aec3fcdcd7ea5a)
- A vendor blog (BigDataBoutique, confidence: low, promotional) claims a single autonomous agent can generate more database queries in an hour than a human analyst team produces in a week, and that agent read/write and concurrency patterns differ structurally from traditional pipelines — directionally consistent with the Berkeley paper's "agentic speculation" framing but not independently verified.
- LLM-driven physical-design/tuning: multiple 2025-2026 papers treat LLMs as index advisors. LLMIA/LLMIdxAdvis (arXiv 2503.07884, 2025) use in-context learning (not fine-tuning) with database feedback to refine recommendations, arguing fine-tuning risks overfitting to a given workload/hardware. A March 2026 paper (arXiv 2603.09181, VLDB 2026) compares LLM-driven tuning against Microsoft's Database Tuning Advisor (DTA) on industrial/enterprise workloads and finds LLM advisors sometimes execute queries substantially faster but with high variance and often worse optimizer-estimated costs than DTA, complicating hybrid advisor designs. — [LLMIA arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.07884v2); [DTA comparison summary](https://rmarcus.info/dbscholar/papers/h9ee2968f11257219)
- NoisePage (CMU, Pavlo lab), the successor to the earlier "self-driving" Peloton project, uses ML for workload forecasting, behavior modeling, and action planning to tune a DBMS without human input (2021 PVLDB paper). A 2022 talk by lead developer Lin Ma (PhD completed under Pavlo, 2021) listed open problems: training-data collection, fast state changes, succinct state/action representations, and accurate reward observation. No 2024-2026 update on NoisePage's status was found in search results — it is unclear whether the project is still actively developed. — [PVLDB 2021 paper](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/volumes/14/paper/Make%20Your%20Database%20System%20Dream%20of%20Electric%20Sheep%3A%20Towards%20Self-Driving%20Operation); [IBM Pavlo interview](https://www.ibm.com/think/insights/database-deep-dives-with-andy-pavlo)
- fsync/durability: a 2021 ACM paper, "Can Applications Recover from fsync Failures?" (Rebello et al.), is the main peer-reviewed source found on whether applications correctly handle fsync failures — this predates the 2020-2026 window and is background only. A 2025-era practitioner post notes that file-content fsync and directory-entry fsync are separate operations, and a file can be lost after a crash even if its own contents were fsynced but the directory entry wasn't. — [SQLite forum](https://sqlite.org/forum/forumpost/5c45279353bc7b7d?raw); [DEV Community post](https://dev.to/javimosch/my-database-claimed-crash-safety-kill-9-proved-nothing-4bp)
- LLM-generated application code's effect on schema/query design: evidence is thin. The most rigorous source found is a March 2026 Cornell preprint studying 376 databases, finding that natural-language-to-SQL generated queries follow a power-law-like template distribution (70% of tested queries covered by just 13% of template types), which the authors use to argue templated generation may be safer/cheaper than free-form LLM SQL generation for routine access (confidence: medium — single preprint, not yet peer-reviewed as far as found). No rigorous empirical study of LLM-generated code's effect on schema design itself was found; remaining sources are vendor/practitioner blogs making unsourced claims (e.g., "changing a schema after production costs 10x more"). — [arXiv 2603.25568](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.25568)

### Inferences
- DST's trajectory (FoundationDB internal tool → TigerBeetle/VOPR → commercial product Antithesis → adopted by a SQLite-rewrite project, Limbo) shows it has crossed from a research curiosity into a standard expectation for new storage-engine projects that make strong correctness claims, directly displacing "we tested it with Jepsen/fault injection" as sufficient evidence — this is a genuine methodological shift in how OLTP/embedded engine correctness is argued for, not just tested.
- The interactive-transactions debate shows an asymmetry worth flagging explicitly: the empirical challenge to "interactive transactions matter" is real and well-evidenced for one workload class (ORM-driven open-source web apps) but a credible critique shows it does not generalize to enterprise/ad hoc/DBA workloads or to non-deterministic systems (Postgres, Spanner) that never needed the assumption. Treat "interactive transactions considered harmful" as a live, contested claim, not a settled finding, as of 2026.
- The LLM-era rethinks (agent-first data systems, MCP proxies, 80%-of-Neon-DBs-by-agents) point at a plausible genuine challenge to "open-world ad hoc queries" and "data independence" as the right interface model — agents generate narrow, bursty, redundant, steerable query loads rather than ad hoc human analyst queries — but as of October 2026 this is mostly vision-paper and vendor-anecdote stage, not yet validated by independent measurement of production agent workloads.

If the report-writer needs a paper with that exact title, further search is needed — it may not exist as a formal title.
- No successor to the 2022 Seattle Report (i.e., nothing for 2024/2025/2026) was located; this should be flagged as a real gap in the field's self-assessment cadence, not just a search miss (two separate targeted searches turned up nothing).
- Direct VLDB/SIGMOD keynote content (as opposed to CIDR papers and blog commentary) for 2023-2026 was not searched directly and is a gap.

- NoisePage/self-driving-database status for 2023-2026 is unclear; no recent update found despite this being one of the most-cited "underexplored direction" claims in older surveys.

## What a small team could realistically contribute (synthesis across directions, not a separate search)

### Takeaway
Based on the above, the directions most tractable for a small team in 2026 are (a) empirical/measurement work (the Demirbas critique explicitly calls for exactly this: real engineering-cost data on converting interactive transactions to one-shot form, and a broader non-ORM-biased replication of the 111-app study), (b) DST tooling applied to an existing embedded engine that lacks it (most embedded/local-first engines besides Limbo/TigerBeetle/FoundationDB still rely on conventional fault injection), and (c) small, focused benchmarking of io_uring/CXL/ZDA tradeoffs, since even the most-cited 2025-2026 papers in those areas (io_uring, CXL) explicitly frame their contribution as "it's not a free win, here's exactly when it helps" — a pattern a small team can replicate for a narrower engine or workload.

### Cited Findings
(This section synthesizes claims already cited above rather than introducing new ones; see citations in the preceding three sections for: Demirbas's explicit call for real engineering-cost data on transaction conversion; the io_uring paper's "not a free win" framing; the CXL buffer-pool proposal's early/prototype status.)

### Inferences
- A small team is poorly positioned to contribute to CXL hardware-availability-dependent work (requires hyperscaler-grade hardware access) or to large-scale agent-workload studies (requires production-scale agent traffic, which only Neon-scale vendors currently have), but is well positioned to contribute an independent, non-ORM-biased empirical workload study (the single most concrete, well-specified open request found in this research — Demirbas's own "I'd welcome a broader paper on this" call), or a DST harness retrofit for one specific existing open-source embedded/OLTP engine.

### Gaps
- This section is a synthesis, not independently sourced; the report-writer should treat it as a secondary-level inference rather than a primary finding, and may want a dedicated researcher pass specifically on "who else is already working on X" for each candidate direction before recommending one.
