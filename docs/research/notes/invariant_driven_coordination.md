# Invariant-Driven Coordination as a Replacement for Isolation Levels

## What do I-confluence, Homeostasis, escrow transactions, bounded counters, Indigo/explicit consistency, IPA, CISE/CEC, Hamsaz, Hampa, ECROs, Katara, Q9 and similar do?

### Takeaway
There is a dense, continuous 1986–2022 research lineage — escrow transactions → invariant confluence (I-confluence) → Homeostasis/CISE/Explicit-Consistency → IPA/Hamsaz/Hampa/Katara/ECROs — that all do the same thing the objective describes: take a declared application invariant (uniqueness, FK, numeric bound, custom predicate) and decide, per invariant and per operation, whether it can run coordination-free while never violating it, coordinating only the residual cases. No source found treats "Q9" as an identifiable system; it could not be confirmed as existing prior art (see Gaps).

### Cited Findings
- Bailis, Fekete, Franklin, Ghodsi, Hellerstein, Stoica, "Coordination Avoidance in Database Systems," PVLDB 8(3), VLDB 2015 (arXiv 2014) define invariant confluence (I-confluence): an invariant/operation set is I-confluent iff merging any two reachable, invariant-satisfying states always yields a state that still satisfies the invariant; this is a necessary and sufficient condition for safe coordination-free, available, convergent execution — [arXiv 1402.2237](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1402.2237), [Bailis blog](https://www.bailis.org/blog/when-does-consistency-require-coordination/).
- Their TPC-C case study found 10 of 12 TPC-C invariants are I-confluent under the benchmark's workload transactions, letting a compliant TPC-C implementation run with no synchronous cross-server coordination; their coordination-avoiding prototype reached roughly 12.7M New-Order tx/s on 200 servers, a 25× improvement over prior distributed results — [arXiv 1402.2237](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1402.2237).
- Companion CIDR 2015 paper, Bailis, "The Case for Invariant-Based Concurrency Control," frames using declared application invariants (rather than ANSI isolation levels) to decide when coordination is needed, arguing this can give far better scalability than serializability for many real workloads — [DBScholar listing](https://rmarcus.info/dbscholar/papers/h4c8ac06048e08ac6).
- The blog post explicitly states the limit: "without knowing the application's invariants, the best you can do under a read/write model is serializability," and "if an invariant is not I-confluent, no coordination-free strategy exists" — coordination is still required in those cases. It gives a non-I-confluent example: a commutative counter that must stay non-negative, decrement-only, can reach a deterministic but invariant-violating (negative) state under concurrent decrements — [Bailis blog](https://www.bailis.org/blog/when-does-consistency-require-coordination/).
- Whittaker and Hellerstein, "Interactive Checks for Coordination Avoidance," VLDB 2019, address that the original I-confluence analysis was done by hand; they give an automatically checkable sufficient condition and introduce "segmented" I-confluence, reporting up to 10× linearizable throughput on low-contention workloads — [blog summary](https://blog.acolyer.org/2019/08/28/interactive-checks-for-coordination-avoidance/), [rmarcus.info](https://rmarcus.info/dbscholar/papers/ha85dc2da21a99160).
- O'Neil (Computer Corporation of America, 1986) introduced the escrow method: it lets long-lived transactions make incremental changes to an aggregate quantity while permitting concurrent access by other transactions, by splitting an allowable-change "pool" across participants so each can act locally within its share; the paper notes the method generalizes beyond simple aggregates — [cs.umb.edu/~poneil/EscrowTM.pdf](https://www.cs.umb.edu/~poneil/EscrowTM.pdf).
- Krishnakumar & Bernstein extended escrow to replicated databases: "High Throughput Escrow Algorithms for Replicated Databases" (VLDB 1992) and "Bounded Ignorance: A Technique for Increasing Concurrency in a Replicated System" (ACM TODS 1994) — bibliographic entries confirmed via DBLP, full text not retrieved — [vldb.org/dblp listing](https://vldb.org/dblp/db/indices/a-tree/k/Krishnakumar:Narayanan.html).
- Roy, Kot, Bender, Ding, Hojjat, Koch, Foster, Gehrke, "The Homeostasis Protocol: Avoiding Transaction Coordination Through Program Analysis," SIGMOD 2015: sites run independently under automatically-derived "treaties" bounding how far they may diverge without violating correctness, communicating only when a treaty would be broken; the paper covers treaty optimization per workload and a prototype evaluated on transactional benchmarks — [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-homeostasis-protocol-avoiding-transaction-coordination-through-program-analysis/), [EPFL infoscience](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/219208).
- Balegas, Duarte, Ferreira, Rodrigues, Preguiça, Najafzadeh, Shapiro, "Putting Consistency Back into Eventual Consistency" (Indigo / Explicit Consistency), EuroSys 2015: statically identifies operations whose concurrent execution could violate a declared application invariant; for each conflict, the programmer chooses among (a) avoiding the violation via a reservation system that moves coordination off the critical path, (b) letting the operation proceed and repairing the invariant afterward. Indigo is the middleware, built atop a causally-consistent store, reported to preserve strong invariants at close to eventually-consistent latency in the common case — [EuroSys15 paper](https://www.dpss.inesc-id.pt/~rodrigo/indigo_eurosys15.pdf).
- Balegas et al., "IPA: Invariant-Preserving Applications for Weakly-Consistent Replicated Databases," VLDB 2019 (preprint 2018): a static analysis finds operations that could jointly violate an invariant under concurrency and automatically rewrites/instruments them so invariants hold without coordination in the no-conflict case, falling back to deterministic conflict-resolution policies otherwise; programmers can pick the semantics applied on conflict — [VLDB 2019 PDF](https://www.dpss.inesc-id.pt/~rodrigo/vldb-2019.pdf), [arXiv 1802.08474](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.08474).
- Gotsman, Yang, Ferreira, Najafzadeh, Shapiro, "'Cause I'm Strong Enough: Reasoning about Consistency Choices in Distributed Systems," POPL 2016, give the first proof rule for checking the correctness of weakly-consistent applications, assuming causal consistency and commutativity of non-conflicting operations; implemented as the CISE tool (Najafzadeh, Gotsman, Yang, Ferreira, Shapiro, "The CISE Tool: Proving Weakly-Consistent Applications Correct," PaPoC 2016), with tooling on Z3/Boogie. CISE expresses consistency as each operation acquiring "tokens," where operations with conflicting tokens cannot run concurrently — [Ferreira slides](https://pages.lip6.fr/Marc.Shapiro/workshop-2017-05-03/attachments/ferreira-slides.pdf).
- CISE3 (arXiv 1909.03721 / 2010.06622), "Verifying Weakly Consistent Applications with Why3," re-implements the CISE proof methodology on the Why3 verification platform.
- Burckhardt, Gotsman, Yang, Zawirski, "Replicated Data Types: Specification, Verification, Optimality," POPL 2014, give a general framework (replication-aware simulation relations) for proving replicated data types (counters, LWW registers, multi-value registers, OR-sets) correct and optimal — [cited via search results], full text not retrieved directly.
- Nair, Petri, Shapiro, "Soteria: Invariant Safety for Distributed Applications," PaPoC 2019: a modular (per-operation), sequential-reasoning proof methodology for verifying invariants of replicated, highly-available applications, focused on state-based CRDTs; it builds concurrency control directly into the application's state and is automated with a prototype tool — [arXiv 1903.02759](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1903.02759), [ar5iv HTML](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1903.02759).
- Li, Houshmand, Lesani, "Hampa: Solver-Aided Recency-Aware Replication" (~2020): given a sequential object plus integrity and recency requirements, it has a relational specification language and a syntax-directed analysis that infers optimal staleness bounds, using solvers both statically and embedded at runtime to decide coordination-avoidance conditions dynamically — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7363281/).
- Hamsaz (Houshmand & Lesani; described via the Katara paper and a related dissertation): given a sequential object's specification and integrity properties, it uses SMT solvers over sets/relations to automatically find conflicting and dependent operation pairs, reduces coordination minimization to a graph optimization problem, and synthesizes correct-by-construction message-passing replication protocols. The Katara paper notes both Hamsaz and Hampa aim to find *efficient coordination protocols*, not to eliminate coordination altogether — [Katara arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.12425).
- Laddad, Power, Milano, Cheung, Hellerstein (?) / actually Bornholt-adjacent team, "Katara: Synthesizing CRDTs with Verified Lifting" (PLDI/OOPSLA-era, 2022): fully automated — program synthesis converts a sequential data-type implementation into a CRDT design and automatically verifies correctness (conformance to the sequential reference type plus an ordering constraint resolving non-commutative-operation conflicts) via SMT-encoded, synthesized inductive invariants; it reproduces classic CRDTs and synthesizes new ones from literature specs, fully automatically verified. No evidence found of integration into a production database or CRDT library — it is presented as a research system — [arXiv 2205.12425](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.12425).
- De Porre, Ferreira, Preguiça, Gonzalez Boix, "ECROs: Building Global-Scale Systems from Sequential Code," OOPSLA 2021 (DOI 10.1145/3485484): ECROs (replicated data types) are derived from an existing sequential data type without modifying its implementation, via a distributed specification that declares application semantics as invariants over replicated state; the derived replicated object requires minimal coordination — [OOPSLA 2021 PDF](https://asc.di.fct.unl.pt/~nmp/pubs/oopsla-2021.pdf).
- Balegas et al., "Extending Eventually Consistent Cloud Databases for Enforcing Numeric Invariants" (2015, arXiv 1503.09052) — bounded-counter style approach: partitions the "gap" between a counter's current value and its limit across replicas so each replica can act purely locally within its allotted share, directly descending from the O'Neil escrow method — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1503.09052).
- Sridhar, Sonnino, Kokoris-Kogias, "Stingray: Fast Concurrent Transactions Without Consensus" (arXiv 2501.06531, 2025): a Byzantine-fault-tolerant bounded counter handling commutative operations and comparisons-against-zero for account balances without consensus, explicitly noted as "strictly harder to implement than CRDTs" due to the non-monotonic zero-comparison; plus FastUnlock for resolving conflicting transactions at consensus-protocol latency; targets blockchain/BFT settings, not a classic single-leader or geo-replicated DBMS — [arXiv 2501.06531](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.06531).

### Inferences
- The field converges on exactly the mechanism the objective describes (declare invariant → analyze which operations threaten it → coordinate only those), but almost every system above targets *geo-replicated/eventually-consistent data stores* (Walter/AntidoteDB-style CRDT platforms), not a general-purpose transactional database with arbitrary SQL-like invariants compiled at build time.
- "Automatic" in this literature almost always means "automatic *given* a hand-declared invariant and a hand-chosen data-type/operation set" — fully automatic discovery of invariants from unannotated application code was not found anywhere in these results.

### Gaps
- No reliable source confirms a system named "Q9" relevant to invariant confluence or coordination avoidance; search queries combining "Q9" with invariant/VLDB/coordination terms returned nothing on-topic. This should be treated as unconfirmed/possibly a misremembered name rather than reported as a gap in the prior art itself.
- Could not retrieve full text of Katara's PLDI/OOPSLA venue and exact author list beyond the abstract page; venue/author details above should be double-checked against the published paper (arXiv 2205.12425 abstract page was read, not the full PDF).

## Which of these are automatic (static analysis of real code) vs. manual annotation, and which were ever built into a production or full database engine vs. a prototype/library?

### Takeaway
Every system found is at most a research prototype or a library layered on a replicated-data-store (chiefly the AntidoteDB/Walter CRDT-store lineage); none was found to be built into a production, general-purpose database engine (e.g., Postgres, MySQL, CockroachDB, Spanner). The "automatic" ones (Katara, Hamsaz, Hampa, Whittaker/Hellerstein's checker, IPA's analysis) still require a manually supplied sequential specification, integrity/invariant statement, or candidate data type — none statically discovers invariants from unannotated, ordinary transactional application code.

### Cited Findings
- I-confluence itself (Bailis et al. 2014/2015) was originally checked manually, case by case for each invariant; Whittaker & Hellerstein (VLDB 2019) built the first automatable/interactive decision procedure and sufficient condition, still requiring the invariant to be declared and the operations modeled — [acolyer.org summary](https://blog.acolyer.org/2019/08/28/interactive-checks-for-coordination-avoidance/).
- Katara is described as automated end-to-end *given* a sequential reference implementation: it synthesizes the CRDT and automatically verifies it via SMT-encoded invariants, with no production-database integration mentioned — [arXiv 2205.12425](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.12425).
- Hamsaz and Hampa both require the developer to supply the object's sequential specification and declared integrity/recency properties; from there the solver-based analysis (set/relation theory for Hamsaz; a relational specification language for Hampa) is automatic — [Katara arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.12425), [Hampa PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7363281/).
- IPA's static analysis automatically finds conflicting operation pairs once the application and its invariants are given, then automatically rewrites the problematic operations; it is a library-level tool evaluated on top of an existing weakly-consistent replicated store, not shipped inside a production DBMS — [VLDB 2019 PDF](https://www.dpss.inesc-id.pt/~rodrigo/vldb-2019.pdf).
- Indigo/Explicit Consistency, Homeostasis, CISE/CISE3, Soteria, and ECROs are all described in their source papers/pages as prototypes or middleware layered on research data stores (causally-consistent stores, CRDT platforms), evaluated via benchmark prototypes — no result found describing adoption inside a shipped, general-purpose production database engine.
- The write-skew/invariant-enforcement patent found (constraint-checks-at-commit, "Constraint-based consistency with snapshot isolation") is a patent filing, not confirmed as implemented in a named shipping product in the sources retrieved — [USPTO PDF](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9805088).

### Inferences
- There is a consistent automatic/manual split across this literature: the *decision procedure* (is this invariant/operation set coordination-free-safe?) is increasingly automatable (Whittaker & Hellerstein, Katara, Hamsaz, Hampa), but the *specification step* (stating the invariant, the sequential semantics, or the candidate data type) remains manual in every system found. A build-time compiler that both discovers invariants from ordinary code and decides coordination needs — with no manual data-type/invariant annotation — was not evidenced anywhere in 2014–2026 prior art surveyed here.
- The absence of production-engine integration is a load-bearing gap: all of escrow's descendants (bounded counters, Indigo, IPA, Hamsaz/Hampa, ECROs, Katara) live either as CRDT libraries/middleware over a replicated key-value/CRDT store, or as standalone academic prototypes benchmarked in isolation.

### Gaps
- Could not confirm or deny whether any closed-source commercial database (e.g., FaunaDB, YugabyteDB, CockroachDB's "fast path" optimizations) has ever shipped an I-confluence-style invariant-driven coordination-avoidance feature; the search surfaced only that CockroachDB defaults to SERIALIZABLE isolation and does not mention invariant-based coordination avoidance — [dbdb.io/CockroachDB discussion threads], no direct confirmation either way.

## How does single-node/embedded use (many cores, one machine) benefit, versus geo-replication only?

### Takeaway
Essentially all retrieved prior art — I-confluence, Homeostasis, Indigo, IPA, CISE, Hamsaz, Hampa, ECROs, Katara — is framed and evaluated exclusively in the geo-replicated / multi-datacenter or replicated-data-store setting; no source was found that applies or evaluates invariant confluence / escrow-style coordination avoidance specifically for single-node, many-core concurrency control within one machine.

### Cited Findings
- A direct search for "invariant confluence single-node many-core concurrency control" returned no 2024-era or any other paper applying I-confluence within one machine across cores; all substantive results concerned distributed/replicated settings (nodes applying transactions locally and merging via gossip, as in the 2026 "Complete CALM" paper) — [search result synthesis above].
- Bailis's CIDR 2015 paper title, "The Case for Invariant-Based Concurrency Control," does gesture at concurrency control generally (not only geo-replication), but the retrieved secondary summary frames its contribution via TPC-C-style distributed/scale-out throughput gains (25× on 200 servers), not single-machine multicore results — [DBScholar listing](https://rmarcus.info/dbscholar/papers/h4c8ac06048e08ac6).
- Stingray (2025) targets a Byzantine, multi-validator (effectively distributed) blockchain setting, not a single-machine many-core scenario, despite being the most recent bounded-counter work found — [arXiv 2501.06531](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.06531).

### Inferences
- The single-node/many-core angle of the objective's "closed-world / workload-compiled database" is apparently *not* where this literature has focused: the entire I-confluence-and-descendants tradition treats "coordination" as cross-replica/cross-datacenter network round trips, and its reported wins (10×–25×, up to 10,000× in Stingray's narrow commutative case) are against distributed consensus/2PC/geo-replication latency, not against single-node lock contention or cache-line conflicts. A candidate claiming single-node many-core benefits from invariant-driven coordination would be building on a different cost model (lock/latch contention and cross-core cache coherency, not WAN RTTs) than any source found here addresses; this looks like a genuine gap in prior art rather than a covered case.

### Gaps
- No source directly evaluates invariant-confluence-style reasoning for single-node, multi-core transaction scheduling (e.g., reducing lock contention or enabling partitioned/sharded execution across cores on one machine using declared invariants). This is a real gap: either such work doesn't exist in the surveyed venues/years, or it uses different terminology (e.g., "deterministic database," "partitioned concurrency control," "OLTP scale-up") that a differently-scoped search would need to find — out of scope for the budget of this research pass.

## Can write skew (the "at least one doctor on call" example) be prevented by checking declared invariants instead of serializable isolation, and is that sound for all invariants? What are the known theoretical limits?

### Takeaway
Yes in principle and partially confirmed: Bailis's own framing explicitly says that without known invariants, serializability is the best a database can do, but I-confluence can safely avoid coordination "even when serializability would indicate otherwise" — however this is NOT sound for all invariants: an invariant that is not I-confluent (the paper's working definition of the limit) requires coordination; the literature's canonical non-confluent example is a non-monotonic bound (e.g., a counter that must stay non-negative under concurrent decrements), and uniqueness/foreign-key-style global constraints were named as the general case likely to fail I-confluence, though a precise citation nailing "uniqueness is never I-confluent" was not retrieved verbatim. The specific "at least one doctor on call" write-skew example from Bailis et al.'s own exposition was not found discussed directly in the sources retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- Bailis's blog is explicit: "without knowing the application's invariants, the best you can do under a read/write model is serializability," and I-confluence "can avoid coordination in many cases even when serializability would indicate otherwise" — but "if an invariant is not I-confluent, no coordination-free strategy exists," meaning coordination is still required in those cases — [Bailis blog](https://www.bailis.org/blog/when-does-consistency-require-coordination/).
- The canonical non-I-confluent example given: a commutative, decrement-only counter that must remain non-negative can reach a deterministic but invariant-violating (negative) state purely from concurrent decrements with no coordination — illustrating that purely local/commutative operations are not sufficient to guarantee an invariant under concurrency — [Bailis blog](https://www.bailis.org/blog/when-does-consistency-require-coordination/).
- Fekete, Cahill, Röhm, "Serializable Isolation for Snapshot Databases," SIGMOD 2008, establish that before their work the *only* known way to prevent write-skew anomalies (the "two on-call doctors" scenario's general class) under snapshot isolation was application-level changes — introducing artificial locking or deliberate update conflicts; their contribution was runtime (not static-invariant) detection of the dangerous read/write pattern inside the DBMS — [Fekete SIGMOD 2008 PDF](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/csep544/09sp/resources/fekete-sigmod2008.pdf).
- A 2024 arXiv critique of snapshot isolation notes that pre-commit validation of a constraint, done independently per transaction, is not sufficient to prevent write skew: "even if each transaction validates the constraint before its commit, two concurrent transactions could still violate the constraint" — a check only helps if it is itself coordinated across the concurrently-committing transactions — [arXiv 2405.18393](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.18393).
- A USPTO patent application ("Constraint-based consistency with snapshot isolation") claims a commit-time check against the application's declared constraints, aborting a transaction when a concurrent update would violate one, with claimed lower overhead and fewer unnecessary aborts than serializable snapshot isolation — a patent's own claim, not independently verified as shipped or benchmarked by a third party — [USPTO PDF](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9805088).
- The Bailis et al. framework's working definition implies a known theoretical limit in general form: any invariant for which merging two independently invariant-satisfying states can produce a state that violates the invariant is not I-confluent and therefore cannot be safely made coordination-free under any protocol — this is stated as the paper's central necessary-and-sufficient result — [arXiv 1402.2237](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1402.2237).

### Inferences
- Declared-invariant checking is a strictly more precise (never worse, often much better) substitute for a global serializable-isolation default, but it is not a free lunch: for genuinely non-I-confluent invariants — intuitively, any invariant that depends on a *global aggregate or global uniqueness* property that two independently-valid partial updates can jointly break — the theory says coordination remains mandatory, and no invariant-checking trick evades that. This matches the objective's own phrasing ("global uniqueness") as a known hard case, consistent with (but not verbatim sourced from) the retrieved material.
- The two 2024/2008 sources on write skew specifically (Fekete et al. 2008, the 2024 SI critique) independently corroborate that *naive* constraint checking (checking a predicate locally at commit without coordinating with concurrent transactions) is unsound for write-skew-class invariants — coordination (or an equivalent commit-order serialization point) is unavoidable for exactly the invariants I-confluence would also flag as non-confluent. This is a convergent, cross-validated finding from two independent literatures (database isolation-level research and the invariant-confluence research line).

### Gaps
- Could not find a source directly discussing the specific "at least one doctor on call" example in connection with I-confluence or any named invariant-confluence system; this classic write-skew example appears generically in isolation-level literature (Fekete et al.) but its direct analysis via I-confluence/declared-invariant checking specifically was not located in this search pass.
- Could not retrieve a precise, citable statement from the Bailis et al. paper itself (full PDF was not deeply parsed beyond search-snippet summaries) proving "uniqueness constraints are never I-confluent" as a general theorem; this is asserted in secondary summaries as "generally fails I-confluence" but a verbatim theorem citation was not confirmed — flagged explicitly as unconfirmed rather than stated as fact.

## Recent (2019–2026) work on verified or invariant-aware databases

### Takeaway
Post-2019 work continues mostly in the CRDT/replicated-data-type verification space (CISE3/Why3, Soteria, Katara, Hamsaz/Hampa descendants, ECROs) plus newer geo-replication-latency work (Stingray 2025, Event Horizon CIDR 2026) and a broadening formal-theory line (Complete CALM, 2026) — but no source found describes a general-purpose, "workload-compiled" relational/SQL database that statically derives per-invariant coordination requirements from ordinary transactional application code at build time, which appears to be the gap the objective's candidate system would fill.

### Cited Findings
- CISE3 (2019/2020, arXiv 1909.03721 and 2010.06622): re-implements the CISE weak-consistency verification methodology on the Why3 proof platform.
- Katara (2022, arXiv 2205.12425): automated synthesis + SMT verification of CRDTs from sequential data-type specs, reproducing classic designs and synthesizing new ones; evaluated as a standalone research system.
- Nair, Petri, Shapiro, "Soteria" (PaPoC 2019, arXiv 1903.02759): modular, sequential-reasoning proof methodology for invariant safety of replicated applications, automated with a prototype tool.
- De Porre et al., "ECROs" (OOPSLA 2021): derives replicated data types with minimal coordination from unmodified sequential code plus a distributed specification declaring invariants over replicated state.
- "LoRe: A Programming Model for Verifiably Safe Local-First Software" (arXiv 2304.07133, 2023) references a related static analysis that repairs transaction/invariant conflicts without added cross-device synchronization, and separately notes that "Gotsman et al. propose an alternative formalization and proof rule which allows reasoning about the invariant-safety of database transactions" — situating LoRe as continuing the CISE-era formalization line into local-first software — [arXiv 2304.07133](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.07133).
- Sridhar, Sonnino, Kokoris-Kogias, "Stingray" (arXiv 2501.06531, 2025): BFT bounded counters and FastUnlock for conflict resolution at consensus latency, in a blockchain/BFT multi-validator setting — the most recent escrow/bounded-counter-lineage work found.
- Arns et al., "Event Horizon: Asymmetric Dependencies for Fast Geo-Distributed Operations," CIDR 2026: confirmed to exist via a paper-index listing (author J. Arns et al., KTH), but its technical content (specific invariant/coordination mechanism) could not be retrieved in this pass — flagged as a promising lead needing direct retrieval, not a confirmed finding — [CIDR 2026 index](https://rankdblp.com/venues/db_conf_cidr_cidr2026_html.html).
- "Complete CALM: A Coordination Criterion for Specifications" (arXiv 2602.09435, 2026 per its arXiv id) is reported to recover I-confluence as an instance of a broader coordination criterion, in a model of nodes applying transactions locally and merging via gossip — content not deeply verified beyond the search-result summary — [search result synthesis above].
- "Sal: Multi-modal Verification of Replicated Data Types" (arXiv 2603.27202) and "VeriFx" (ECOOP 2023) appeared as newer verification-tool leads in search results but their content was not retrieved in this pass.

### Inferences
- The 2019–2026 trajectory shows the field steadily automating *verification* of hand-specified invariants/data types (CISE3, Soteria, Katara) and pushing into new deployment settings (BFT/blockchain with Stingray, local-first software with LoRe, geo-distributed asymmetric dependencies with Event Horizon) — but the core framing never generalizes from "replicated data type / CRDT with a declared invariant" to "arbitrary relational schema with declared business invariants, compiled once at build time to decide per-transaction coordination," which is the specific novelty claim the objective is probing. No contradicting evidence (i.e., no source claiming this exact generalization already exists) was found, which is itself informative but should be read as absence-of-evidence from a time-boxed search, not proof of absence.

### Gaps
- Several promising 2026 leads (Event Horizon CIDR 2026, Complete CALM, Sal) were identified by title/venue only; their full technical content was not retrieved within this research pass's tool budget. A follow-up pass fetching these three papers directly would materially strengthen confidence in the "no general build-time invariant-coordination compiler found" conclusion.
- No systematic recent (2023-2026) survey or comparison paper of this entire I-confluence/CRDT-verification lineage was found or read in full; the picture above is assembled from individual paper leads rather than a single authoritative survey, so coverage of 2024-2025 work in particular may be incomplete.
